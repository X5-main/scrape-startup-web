Hiring a Law Firm vs. Independent Lawyer: What’s Better?

General Legal Team
August 11, 2026

If you’re going the traditional route for legal help, you’ll usually end up choosing between two options: a law firm or an independent lawyer.

The market itself doesn’t make the call easy. It’s nearly an even split, as solo practitioners hold 49% of the field, while the other 51% is carved up between small, midsize, and BigLaw firms.

On paper, both of these categories solve the same legal problems. In practice, they often deliver very different experiences. To help you choose the right fit for your needs, this guide on hiring a law firm vs. independent lawyer compares those experiences across eight key areas.

Key takeaways

  • Choose based on the complexity of your legal needs
    Law firms are often better for matters that require multiple specialists, while independent lawyers are usually a strong fit for focused legal issues.
  • Cost structures can vary significantly
    Independent lawyers often offer more flexible pricing, while traditional firms tend to have higher overhead and hourly rates.
  • Direct access comes with tradeoffs
    Solo practitioners typically provide more direct communication and personal attention, but law firms offer broader support and backup coverage.
  • Resources and continuity matter
    Larger firms can draw on teams, support staff, and specialized expertise, making them better equipped for complex or time-sensitive matters.
  • AI-native law firms offer a third option
    Firms like General Legal combine experienced attorneys with AI-powered workflows, giving businesses access to senior legal talent, predictable pricing, and firm-level resources without many of the traditional inefficiencies.

Hiring a law firm vs. independent lawyer: 8 key differences

While you’re hiring lawyers in either scenario, these eight differences will dictate how your matter is staffed, coordinated, and executed.

1. What you’ll pay and how

As many as 46% of Americans who don’t seek legal help cite cost concerns as a primary reason for foregoing representation. That’s why one of the first questions people ask is how much it costs to hire a lawyer.

Generally speaking, solo practitioners are perceived as the more affordable option.

The logic is straightforward: There are fewer employees, less office space, and lower overhead. In addition, independent lawyers are often more flexible with fee arrangements. Since they can make pricing decisions on their own terms, these lawyers can offer flat fees, capped fees, unbundled services, or other arrangements that wouldn’t fit as easily within a firm’s pricing model.

Law firms, meanwhile, tend to charge more because you’re paying for more than one lawyer. There’s support staff, associates, partners, office infrastructure, knowledge management systems, and the administrative machinery required to run a larger operation.

The price difference between the two can become substantial at the top end of the market. While lawyers charged an average of $349 per hour in 2025, standard rates at Am Law 100 firms crossed the $1,000-per-hour mark. As for lawyers outside that elite tier, they averaged closer to $600 per hour.

However, there are exceptions.

An experienced solo practitioner with a strong reputation, niche expertise, or a highly specialized practice can charge rates that rival those of major firms.

The opposite is also true, as not all law firms are more expensive than independent lawyers.

The rise of AI-native law firms has introduced a different approach to billing. Firms like General Legal combine experienced attorneys with AI-powered workflows to eliminate much of the administrative and repetitive work that traditionally gets billed to clients.

Because these firms often operate with lower overhead than traditional firms and different cost structures than firms burdened by significant law firm startup costs, this approach can translate into lower and more predictable pricing.

2. What expertise you’ll have access to

Legal issues rarely stay confined to a single area of law. For example, a single acquisition can involve intellectual property, privacy, regulatory compliance, and corporate governance at the same time.

Law firms are typically better positioned for these complex cases because they bring together specialists from multiple practice areas under one roof. This arrangement creates an often-overlooked advantage: knowledge-sharing.

Because partners share in the firm’s overall success, they have a strong incentive to pull in the best internal specialist for a particular issue. This leads to a smoother transfer of information and less friction than starting over with a completely new legal provider.

Independent lawyers tend to operate differently.

Some are highly specialized and focus on a narrow area of law, while others act as generalists serving a broader range of client needs. Both approaches can work well, but when a legal issue extends beyond the lawyer’s core expertise, additional outside counsel may be needed.

While independent lawyers can—and do—maintain strong professional networks, these referrals typically happen across separate businesses rather than within a single organization. This makes collaboration and information-sharing less seamless when multiple areas of expertise are required.

3. How connected you’ll be to your legal team

Communication shapes every aspect of the client experience, from how quickly questions are answered to how effectively legal strategy is explained and executed.

When it comes to law firms, communication may pass through multiple people, depending on the size of the firm and nature of the engagement. This includes:

  • Partners
  • Associates
  • Paralegals
  • Client service staff

While this structured approach to communication can improve coordination on larger matters, it also creates more opportunities for delays, misunderstandings, and communication gaps. These challenges may help explain why 79% of law firm clients report feeling uncared for during their engagement. 

Independent lawyers generally offer a more direct line of communication, with questions, updates, and strategic discussions happening with the same person handling the work. By minimizing intermediaries, they also reduce the risk of information being lost or miscommunicated along the way.

4. Who will actually do the work

When hiring a law firm, the lawyer you meet during the sales process isn’t always the lawyer managing the day-to-day work.

In many firms, senior partners focus on client relationships, legal strategy, and business development, while associates are in charge of legal research, document drafting, due diligence, discovery, and routine client communications. This staffing model can be efficient, but it can also mean that much of the work is performed by lawyers with less experience than clients initially anticipated.

That’s not the case with independent lawyers.

The person you hire is normally the person reviewing documents, answering questions, developing strategy, and carrying out the legal work. For clients who specifically want a senior lawyer managing the work throughout the engagement, this model can be a significant advantage.

That said, delegation isn’t inherently negative.

Many associates are highly capable lawyers, and leveraging a mix of senior and junior professionals can reduce costs by reserving partner time for higher-value work.

5. How much personal attention you’ll receive

Since solo practitioners typically manage smaller caseloads and handle matters personally from start to finish, they can devote more attention to each client. Over time, this continuity can lead to a stronger working relationship and a deeper understanding of the client’s goals.

As for law firms, personalization is often more dependent on how the matter is staffed.

If portions of your case are delegated to team members, the lawyer leading the engagement may pay less attention to your matter. As a result, recommendations may be informed by less context than they would be in a more hands-on arrangement.

6. How your legal strategy is shaped

Independent lawyers often have greater flexibility in how they approach a legal issue. Without firm-wide policies, approval chains, or standardized workflows, they can tailor their strategy more closely to a client’s specific objectives and risk tolerance.

Law firms tend to rely on more structured processes.

That consistency can be beneficial, particularly for recurring matters or complex engagements that require coordination across multiple people. However, it can also make legal strategies less adaptable to a client’s specific circumstances, particularly when a firm’s established processes take precedence over a more customized approach.

7. How much continuity you’ll have

Legal work rarely follows a predictable schedule. A court deadline can move up, an opposing party can send an urgent demand, or a deal that seemed dormant can suddenly require immediate review. In those moments, responsiveness and backup coverage become just as important as legal expertise.

Law firms are generally better equipped to maintain continuity in these situations.

If one attorney is unavailable, another lawyer or member of the support team can always take over. This is particularly valuable in time-sensitive matters where delays can carry legal or financial consequences, such as:

  • Responding to a demand letter to avoid escalation or litigation
  • Reviewing acquisition documents before a scheduled closing date
  • Meeting court-imposed deadlines, where a missed filing can result in the loss of important legal rights
  • Addressing a lender’s requests during a financing process to prevent funding delays

Independent lawyers typically have less built-in coverage.

Since these lawyers are usually the sole point of contact, there may be no one else with enough context to step in immediately. So, if they’re attending trial, taking a vacation, dealing with a personal emergency, or just operating at capacity, work may stop until they’re available again.

8. What resources you’ll have available

Many matters require far more than legal advice alone. Depending on the case, they may involve document review, legal research, drafting, filing, fact gathering, and coordination with outside parties.

Law firms typically support this work with a broader operational structure, which may include:

  • Paralegals and legal assistants
  • Administrative and operations staff
  • Litigation support personnel
  • Relationships with expert witnesses and outside consultants
  • Internal research resources and document libraries

Independent lawyers, on the other hand, handle a larger share of these responsibilities personally. This can create a leaner operation, but it also means fewer resources are available when a legal issue requires significant support beyond the attorney’s own time.

However, this dynamic may change over time as more legal professionals learn how to use AI in law effectively. As of 2025, AI adoption reached 71% among solo practitioners and climbed to 87% at firms with over 50 employees.

Thanks to AI, tasks that once required teams of paralegals, researchers, and support staff can now be completed much faster, allowing lawyers to accomplish more with fewer people involved.

As these AI-powered workflows become standard across the profession, the resource gap between independent lawyers and law firms may narrow. With the lines between these two options blurring, how should you make your final decision?

Hiring a law firm vs. independent lawyer: Which way should you go?

The right choice between a law firm and an independent lawyer ultimately depends on the type of legal issue you’re facing.

If you’re handling a merger, acquisition, financing round, major regulatory matter, or another project that touches multiple areas of law, a law firm will usually be the stronger choice. These matters often require coordinated input from different specialists, and having those capabilities available within a single organization can make complex projects easier to manage.

Law firms are also a good fit for litigation involving large volumes of documents, multiple parties, or substantial financial exposure. In these situations, the additional personnel and support infrastructure can be just as important as the legal advice itself.

Independent lawyers tend to be a strong choice when the issue is more focused and self-contained. Examples include engagements where success depends primarily on the judgment and expertise of a single practitioner, such as:

  • Estate planning
  • Real estate transactions
  • Criminal defense
  • Family law matters

Solo practitioners can also be a great fit for clients who already know exactly what type of legal help they need and want to build a direct, long-term relationship with the lawyer providing it. In the business world, this relationship can evolve into an outside general counsel arrangement, with the same lawyer advising on day-to-day legal issues.

Of course, this doesn’t mean independent lawyers have a monopoly on long-term legal relationships for startups and high-growth companies. A new generation of outside general counsel law firms aims to combine the affordability and accessibility that attract clients to independent lawyers with the broader resources and expertise of a firm.

FAQ

What is a general lawyer?

A general lawyer is a lawyer who handles a broad range of legal issues instead of focusing on a single specialty.

How do you choose a lawyer with confidence?

To choose a lawyer with confidence, look for one with relevant experience, clear communication systems, transparent pricing, and a track record of handling matters similar to yours.

What does a commercial law firm do?

A commercial law firm helps businesses with contracts, transactions, employment issues, regulatory compliance, and other legal matters that arise during day-to-day operations.

What are the best law firms for startups?

The best law firms for startups are those with experience advising high-growth companies, transparent pricing, responsive communication, and expertise in areas like venture capital, commercial contracts, employment, and data privacy. Many startups also consider AI-native law firms, which use technology to deliver legal services more efficiently while maintaining attorney oversight.

What an AI meeting assistant actually is

Most AI meeting assistants only start working when the audio does. An AI meeting assistant records your calls, transcribes them, labels who said what, and turns the result into notes you can search later. It captures the audio, produces a transcript, attributes lines to the people who said them, and turns that into a summary you can read and search. Some tools do this by sending a bot into the meeting as a visible participant. Others record the audio on your own machine, so nothing joins the call.

The category grew out of transcription. That heritage still shows: plenty of tools stop once the words are on the page. The gap that matters is what you can do with the notes afterward, in the follow-up email, the recap, the answer someone asks you for a month later.

Wispr Notetaker records on your device instead of joining as a bot. The meeting looks normal because nothing joins it. Always tell everyone on the call that you're recording.

Transcript, notes, readout, assistant: what is the difference?

These four words get used as if they mean the same thing. They don't, and the difference decides which tool you need.

  • Transcript: the words, in order, ideally with a name next to each line. It is insurance. You go back to it when something is disputed or you need an exact quote
  • Notes: what you or the tool wrote down as it happened. Yours carry judgment about what mattered; a tool's carry coverage
  • Readout: the short version someone else can read, organized by topic, with what was decided and what happens next
  • Assistant: the layer that answers questions across all of it, and gets your meetings into the other tools you work in

A tool that only produces a transcript is a recorder. A tool that produces a readout you can hand to a colleague, and answers questions across months of calls, is doing the assistant job. Most products sit somewhere in between, and the honest way to compare them is to ask which of those four they actually deliver.

What happens before the meeting

Most meeting tools do nothing until the audio starts. The before-the-call half of this category is the newer half, and it is where tools differ most.

Wispr Notetaker builds a brief: a short, scannable rundown ahead of each meeting, put together from your Gmail, Slack, and past notes, plus research from the web, like someone's LinkedIn or what they've published. Each line links back to its source, so you can click through when a line matters. For a recurring meeting, it shows what was decided last time and what stayed open.

The same context does quiet work during capture. Wispr Notetaker goes into the meeting knowing who is on the invite and knowing the terms in your dictionary, which is why unusual names and internal acronyms come out spelled correctly instead of phonetically. Context gathered before the call is what makes the transcript after it worth reading.

What happens during and after the meeting

During the call, you get a live transcript. Speakers show as You and Them while it runs. You can also ask what you missed while the call is still running, and the answer comes back in chat. That matters most when you joined late.

After the meeting, Wispr Notetaker rechecks its own transcript. It then labels speakers from the calendar invite, your connected apps, and clues in the conversation. Somebody says "what do you think, Dan?" and that is enough to place a speaker. Anyone it can't place, you name once and the label applies across the whole transcript.

That refined transcript feeds the summary, organized by topic with next steps for everyone in the meeting. You can also ask questions in plain language across your meeting history and the emails, threads, and events around it. Every answer links to the source, down to the moment it came from. And you can pull your meetings into Claude, ChatGPT, or Copilot without copying and pasting anything.

What to look for when you pick one

Six things separate tools in this category. Check them in this order.

  1. Capture method. A bot is a visible participant everyone sees; device recording is not. A bot can only join the platforms it supports, so check the list against the platforms you use
  2. Speaker attribution. Ask what the final transcript says on the platform you actually use. Some tools name people only in certain apps and fall back to generic labels everywhere else
  3. Custom vocabulary. Your product names, your customers' names, your acronyms. Check whether the tool learns them or whether you type them into a box by hand
  4. History access on the plan you'll be on. How far back you can read on the plan you'll actually be on. Some tools keep recording without limit but lock older notes behind an upgrade, so check the retention window, not just the recording allowance
  5. AI-tool access. Whether your notes can be read by Claude or ChatGPT, and whether that is limited to summaries or covers full transcripts
  6. Platform coverage. Mac, Windows, mobile, and whether in-person and ad-hoc calls are handled at all

Who this category suits, and who it doesn't

An AI meeting assistant earns its place if your week is mostly calls and the work happens after them: recaps to write, decisions to relay, quotes to pull. It suits people piping meetings into Claude or ChatGPT, where one wrong speaker label spoils everything downstream, and people who need precise words, like journalists and researchers.

It is a poor fit if your meetings are rare, short, or so sensitive that no recording is appropriate. It is also a poor fit if you want the tool to do the follow-up work itself. These tools produce notes; you still act on them.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a bot to record a meeting?

No. A bot is one capture method, not a requirement. Tools like Wispr Notetaker record the audio on your device, so nothing joins the call as a participant. That also means capture isn't limited to the platforms a bot supports, so Zoom, Google Meet, Teams, and a Slack huddle all work the same way.

Can an AI meeting assistant record an in-person meeting?

It depends on the capture method. A bot needs a call to join, so it can't cover a room. Wispr Notetaker records on your device, and it detects calls that were never on your calendar and offers a one-tap start, so an unplanned conversation at your desk can be captured the same way a scheduled call is.

Can I use my meeting notes inside Claude or ChatGPT?

Yes with Wispr Notetaker, on Free and Pro. Your transcripts, summaries, and notes can be pulled into Claude, ChatGPT, or Copilot without copying and pasting. Ask Claude to recap today's meetings and it will, because your notes are right there.

What happens if the transcript doesn't know who someone is?

Wispr Notetaker labels speakers after the meeting using your calendar invite, your connected apps, and clues in the conversation. Anyone it can't identify, you name once after the call and that label applies across the whole transcript. During the meeting, speakers show as You and Them.

Does Wispr Notetaker keep recordings of my meetings?

Your meetings are yours. Meeting audio is encrypted and kept only temporarily to create your transcript, let you resume a meeting, verify quality, and troubleshoot. After that limited period it's automatically removed. Wispr Notetaker doesn't create voiceprints or biometric profiles of you or anyone on your call.

Does Wispr Notetaker train AI models on my meetings?

Your data is never used for model training unless you opt in, and you can opt out at any time. You can also delete any meeting, transcript, or note whenever you want. Wispr Notetaker is HIPAA-ready across tiers, with a HIPAA-ready BAA on Enterprise.
